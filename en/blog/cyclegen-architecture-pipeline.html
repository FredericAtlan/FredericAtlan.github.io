CycleGen — driving cycles from a map

Draw a route, pick a vehicle and a driving style: CycleGen produces the driving cycle that feeds the models used to size brakes, regeneration and the battery. The commercial tools I used for this cost tens of thousands of euros a year. CycleGen is open source, and the demo is waiting at the end of the article.

Why a custom cycle

A vehicle simulator runs on speed, time and grade profiles. A flat regulatory cycle never shows you the pass descent, and that is where the brakes heat up and where an electric vehicle's regeneration saturates. To size these systems you need cycles that look like real use, and you need to know which operating points come up often.

CycleGen starts from a route on a map. It reads the speed limits (OpenStreetMap, OSRM). Road curvature caps cornering speed through a lateral comfort limit. For traffic lights, major roads get a green wave; elsewhere, red or green is drawn with a fixed seed, so the same route always gives the same cycle. With a Mapbox key, you can add traffic slowdowns based on the departure time.

The driver has three profiles (Eco, Normal, Sport) that set how hard they accelerate and brake. On the vehicle side, the F0, F1, F2 road-load law and the mass give the forces, torque and power at the wheels. Every step can be opened in the workbench, from the map to the final cycle, and the result exports to CSV, XML or an Excel workbook.

The tool also ships the reference cycles (WLTC, NEDC, FTP-75, US06…) so you can compare a generated cycle with what you normally use. I have compared generated cycles with measured drives, but I cannot publish those results.

Who uses it

I designed it first for vehicle simulation: brakes, regeneration, battery. It also helps prepare a test-drive campaign, by spotting the defining routes (climbs, descents, traffic-light density) before going out. And a data scientist can call it in batches through its API to build a database of customer driving. Everything goes through the HTTP API, no screen needed.

By default, everything runs on open mapping services. For traffic, you plug in a pay-as-you-go API such as Mapbox, free up to 100,000 route requests a month ($2 per 1,000 beyond that, per its November 2026 price list).

Architecture: what is slow, what is fast

The web screen is a workbench (map, sliders, charts). The driver simulation runs on a Python server, so a script can call it directly. Forces, torque and power are recomputed in the workbench, so switching vehicles is instant.

Rebuilding the geometry (route, elevation, traffic lights) takes time, because it has to query several services and terrain models. Once that corridor is in memory, changing the driver or the vehicle takes a few tens of milliseconds. So the corridor is rebuilt only when you ask for it; tweaking the driver just re-runs the simulation and leaves the map as it is.

Driver

The driver follows an IDM (Intelligent Driver Model), which at each step balances the target speed against a safe following distance. There is no other car ahead: the “lead vehicle” is virtual and stands for the next bend, the next drop in the speed limit or the next red light, which makes the driver anticipate. Eco, Normal and Sport change the maximum acceleration, the comfortable braking, the speed factor, the time headway and the tolerated jerk, all with the same model.

There's an optional small noise term to mimic jitter in free driving. It gets switched off as soon as the driver brakes hard or a hazard is too close.

Equations and code: driver

IDM acceleration and desired gap \(s^*\) (\(a_{\max}\) desired acceleration, \(b_{\mathrm{comf}}\) comfortable braking, \(T\) time headway, \(s_0\) standstill gap):

\[ a_{\mathrm{IDM}}=a_{\max}\left( 1-\left(\frac{v}{v_0}\right)^{\delta} -\left(\frac{s^{*}}{s}\right)^{2} \right) \quad(\delta=4) \] \[ s^{*}=s_0+v\,T+\frac{v\,\Delta v}{2\sqrt{a_{\max}\,b_{\mathrm{comf}}}} \] \[ v_0=\max\bigl(0{,}1,\; V_{\mathrm{envelope}}(s)\cdot \texttt{speed\_factor}\bigr) \]

Noise cut-off and clipping, then Euler integration (\(\Delta t\approx 1\,\mathrm{s}\)):

\[ d_{\mathrm{brake}}=\max\!\Bigl(\frac{v^{2}}{2\,b_{\mathrm{comf}}},\,25\Bigr)\quad[\mathrm{m}] \] \[ a=\operatorname{clip}\bigl(a_{\mathrm{IDM}}+a_{\mathrm{noise}},\; -1{,}5\,b_{\mathrm{comf}},\; a_{\max}\bigr) \] \[ v\leftarrow v+a\,\Delta t,\qquad s\leftarrow s+v_{\mathrm{old}}\,\Delta t \] 
# Excerpt — a_IDM (kinematic_solver.py)
free = 1.0 - (v / v0) ** DELTA
dv = v - v_lead
s_star = params.s0 + max(
    0.0,
    v * params.T
    + (v * dv) / (2.0 * math.sqrt(max(params.a_max * params.b_comf, 1e-6))),
)
return params.a_max * (free - (s_star / gap) ** 2)

Corridor

Before driving, we prepare what lies along the road: grade, speed limits, a traffic estimate when available, comfortable cornering speed. The resulting envelope is the minimum of these limits. Raw elevation sometimes dips under a bridge, so we correct for map structures and smooth the data before computing the grade. The screen shows it in %, the maths use radians.

Equations: corridor and envelope

Object indexed by distance \(s\):

\[ \bigl(S,\;\alpha(s),\;V_{\mathrm{legal}},\;V_{\mathrm{traffic}},\;V_{\mathrm{curve}},\;V_{\mathrm{envelope}},\;S_{\mathrm{feux}}\bigr) \]

Grade after smoothing (Savitzky–Golay, denoted SG):

\[ \alpha_i=\operatorname{atan}\!\left(\frac{z^{\mathrm{SG}}_i-z^{\mathrm{SG}}_{i-1}}{S_i-S_{i-1}}\right) \]

Cornering (\(a_y\) lateral comfort, \(R\) radius) and envelope:

\[ V_{\mathrm{curve}}(s)=\sqrt{a_y\,R(s)} \] \[ V_{\mathrm{envelope}}(s)=\min\bigl(V_{\mathrm{legal}}(s),\,V_{\mathrm{traffic}}(s),\,V_{\mathrm{curve}}(s)\bigr) \]

Forces at the wheels

The driver proposes an acceleration. The vehicle model bounds what traction and brakes can actually deliver: road load (coefficients F0, F1, F2), the grade component, then the applied force limited by power and maximum braking. From that we get the actual acceleration, and from there torque and power at the wheels.

The demo catalog has ballpark figures for electric vehicles, and they are unofficial. If the study matters, use manufacturer data or authorized measurements instead.

Equations and code: force balance \[ F_{\mathrm{need}}=m\,a_{\mathrm{cmd}}+F_{\mathrm{road}}+F_{\mathrm{grade}} \] \[ F_{\mathrm{road}}=F_0+F_1\,v_{\mathrm{km/h}}+F_2\,v_{\mathrm{km/h}}^{2},\qquad F_{\mathrm{grade}}=m\,g\,\sin\alpha \] \[ a=\frac{F_{\mathrm{applied}}-F_{\mathrm{road}}-F_{\mathrm{grade}}}{m} \] \[ C_{\mathrm{w}}=F\,r_{\mathrm{dyn}},\qquad P=C_{\mathrm{w}}\,\omega \] 
# Road-load (kinematic_solver.py)
def _road_load_n(v_mps: float, plant: VehiclePlant) -> float:
    vk = max(0.0, v_mps) * 3.6  # m/s → km/h
    return plant.f0_n + plant.f1_n_per_kph * vk + plant.f2_n_per_kph2 * vk * vk

The mountain test

What I cared about most was the mountains. The demo opens an energy study on a Grossglockner reference trace (14 km, 1,250 m of descent, speed synthesized from the grade and the bends). The screenshots below come from a route that CycleGen computed on Pico Veleta.

One chart, not nine panels

Everything starts from one main chart, usually speed. A strip of thumbnails switches the quantity shown, and a shared cursor links map ↔ curves. I wanted people to look at what is happening (envelope tracking, a torque peak) instead of spending their time rearranging nine panels.

What CycleGen does not do

CycleGen computes the driving profile and the total effort at the wheels, not how that effort is split. The split between friction brakes and regeneration depends on the thermal limits and the state of charge of the powertrain. That is the job of another model, which takes CycleGen profiles as input.

Other limits: it replaces neither a field test campaign nor homologation. It ignores the equivalent mass of rotating parts. In bends, it caps speed with a comfort limit and does not model tire grip. The time step is one second. Finally, the demo server is hosted on Render: after a quiet spell, the first computation can take around twenty seconds.
