CycleGen — driving cycles from a map

Draw a route, pick a vehicle and a driving style: CycleGen produces the driving cycle that feeds brake, regeneration and battery models. The commercial tools that do this job are expensive and closed; this one is open source, and the demo is at the end of the article.

Why I needed custom cycles

The need comes from my work. A vehicle simulator feeds on speed, time and grade profiles. With a flat regulatory cycle you never see a mountain descent, like the one from Pico Veleta down to Granada, yet that is where brakes heat up and where an electric vehicle’s regeneration saturates. To size these systems you need cycles that look like real use, and you need to know which operating points come up often. The tools that produce them do not show their assumptions. So I wrote CycleGen.

The tool also ships the reference cycles (WLTC, NEDC, FTP-75, US06…) to compare a generated cycle with what is usually used. I compared generated cycles with measured drives, but I cannot publish those results.

From map to cycle: what is slow, what is fast

The web screen is a workbench. The driver simulation runs on a Python server, so a script can call it directly. Rebuilding the road takes time, because several services and terrain models have to be queried. Once that road is prepared and kept in memory, changing the driver or the vehicle takes only a few tens of milliseconds: adjusting a setting reruns the simulation without resetting the map.

Preparing the road: grade, curves, maximum speed

Before driving, the server prepares what I call the corridor: the road cut along its distance, with its grade, its speed limits, the comfort speed in each curve and, optionally with a Mapbox key, a traffic estimate for the departure time. For traffic lights, main roads get a green wave; elsewhere, the red or green state is drawn with a fixed seed, so the same route always gives the same cycle.

Raw elevation sometimes dives under a bridge: bridges and tunnels are corrected first, then the profile is smoothed, then the grade is computed over fixed-length steps. The maximum speed kept, the envelope, is the minimum of all these limits, made brakeable and then smoothed.

Corridor and envelope equations

Grade over steps of length \(L\), after a moving average and a light Savitzky–Golay smoothing of elevation \(z\):

\[ \alpha_k=\operatorname{atan}\!\left(\frac{\Delta z_k}{L}\right) \]

Curve (\(a_y\) lateral comfort, \(R\) radius) and envelope:

\[ V_{\mathrm{curve}}(s)=\sqrt{a_y\,R(s)} \] \[ V_{\mathrm{envelope}}(s)=\min\bigl(V_{\mathrm{curve}},\,V_{\max,\mathrm{veh}},\,V_{\mathrm{legal}},\,V_{\mathrm{traffic}}\bigr) \]

\(V_{\mathrm{traffic}}\) only enters the envelope when the traffic option is enabled. The envelope is then made compatible with a realistic braking deceleration, and smoothed.

Simulating the driver with the IDM model

The driver follows an IDM (Intelligent Driver Model), which trades off, every second, between the target speed and the safety distance. There is no other car ahead: the “lead vehicle” is virtual. It stands for the next curve, the next lower limit or the next red light, which makes the driver anticipate. Uphill, the driver compensates the grade to hold speed; downhill, it adds up.

The interface offers three profiles. They share the same model and only change its parameters:

Each profile also sets the standstill gap, the anticipation horizon and the tolerated jerk. Optional small noise simulates nervousness in free driving; it is cut as soon as hard braking is needed or an obstacle is too close.

Driver equations and code

IDM acceleration and desired gap \(s^*\) (\(\Delta v = v - v_{\text{lead}}\), \(a_{\max}\) maximum acceleration, \(b_{\mathrm{comf}}\) comfort braking, \(T\) time headway, \(s_0\) standstill gap):

\[ a_{\mathrm{IDM}}=a_{\max}\left( 1-\left(\frac{v}{v_0}\right)^{\delta} -\left(\frac{s^{*}}{s}\right)^{2} \right) \quad(\delta=4) \] \[ s^{*}=s_0+\max\!\Bigl(0,\;v\,T+\frac{v\,\Delta v}{2\sqrt{a_{\max}\,b_{\mathrm{comf}}}}\Bigr), \qquad v_0=\max\bigl(0.1,\; V_{\mathrm{envelope}}(s)\cdot \texttt{speed\_factor}\bigr) \]

Grade compensated uphill, added downhill:

\[ a_{\mathrm{dyn}}=a_{\mathrm{IDM}}-g\sin\alpha+\max(0,\;g\sin\alpha) \]

Comfort bounds, then a jerk limit, and fixed-step Euler integration (\(\Delta t=1\,\mathrm{s}\)):

\[ a=\operatorname{clip}\bigl(a_{\mathrm{dyn}}+a_{\mathrm{noise}},\;-b_{\mathrm{comf}},\; a_{\max}\bigr) \] \[ v\leftarrow v+a\,\Delta t,\qquad s\leftarrow s+v\,\Delta t \] 
# Excerpt — a_IDM (kinematic_solver.py)
free = 1.0 - (v / v0) ** DELTA
dv = v - v_lead
s_star = params.s0 + max(
    0.0,
    v * params.T
    + (v * dv) / (2.0 * math.sqrt(max(params.a_max * params.b_comf, 1e-6))),
)
return params.a_max * (free - (s_star / gap) ** 2)

From speeds to wheel force, torque and power

The cycle comes out of the server; the workbench derives, in the browser, what the wheels must deliver. The force combines rolling and aerodynamic resistance (road-load coefficients F0, F1, F2), the grade component and inertia. The wheel radius gives the torque, the speed gives the power. Switching vehicles is therefore instant.

The demo catalogue offers orders of magnitude for electric vehicles, not official figures. For a study that matters, replace them with manufacturer data or authorised measurements.

A mountain route: Pico Veleta

Back to Pico Veleta. The screenshots in this article come from that route, computed by CycleGen down to Granada.

To read the result, everything starts from one main chart, usually speed. A strip of thumbnails switches the quantity shown, and a shared cursor links the map and the curves. On long descents, the force demanded at the wheels turns negative: that is the energy the brakes and regeneration share between them.

The aim is to keep your eyes on the phenomenon, without having to rearrange nine panels.

Under the hood

By default, everything runs on open mapping services. For traffic, you plug in a pay-as-you-go API such as Mapbox, free up to a monthly quota; HERE can supply the route, speed limits and elevation. Without a key, the server falls back to OSRM.

What CycleGen does not do

CycleGen computes the driving profile and the total effort at the wheels, not how it is split. Sharing between the mechanical brake and regeneration depends on the powertrain’s thermal limits and state of charge: that is the job of another model, which takes CycleGen’s profiles as input. The solver can cap the force by the maximum power and brake torque, but that limit is not wired in yet: the workbench shows the force required, without capping it.

It does not replace a field campaign or homologation. It ignores the equivalent mass of rotating parts. In curves, it bounds speed by comfort, without modelling grip. The time step is one second. Finally, the demo server is hosted on Render: after a period of inactivity, the first computation can take about twenty seconds.

Try the demo

Limited demo. The online version runs without a Mapbox or HERE key: no real traffic, and the route, speed limits and elevation come only from open services (OSRM, OpenStreetMap, Open-Meteo, OpenTopoData). To use your own Mapbox and HERE keys, get the full code on my GitHub.

The demo also opens on a ready-made study: a descent of the Grossglockner (14 km, 1,250 m of elevation loss), whose speed is synthesised from grade and curves.
