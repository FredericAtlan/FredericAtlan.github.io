STTNS — so that no line of code is written for nothing

Do you get priorities from a CEO or a product manager and have to turn them into development cycles you can defend? STTNS ties every story to the pain of a real user, lets Product put a number on value and Tech on cost, then divides one by the other. A feature with no declared value does not start. I designed it at Data Refiners for a small team of experts; it is open source and the demo runs on your own machine.

Why I built it

Leading a handful of experts means deciding every week where their time goes. In a spreadsheet you quickly lose track: who asked for this feature, for which customer, with what expected benefit? The ticketing tools on the market track a ticket’s progress very well, the reason it exists much less so, and setting them up would have cost us more time than we had.

I also needed a teaching tool. Starting from the personas’ pain points and their jobs-to-be-done (JTBD, what the user is trying to get done) is something everyone agrees with in a meeting, and far fewer teams actually practise. I wanted the method to be visible on screen, without having to remind people of it at every team meeting.

At first I meant to implement the Straight to the North Star framework, hence the name. I did, then found that the North Star Metric, the single metric that acts as a compass, fitted our work poorly. We do software development led by experts, and the value of a week’s work does not fit in one number. The current framework came out of team discussions around a simple question: how do we choose what to code so that we do not code what nobody needs.

For us, the result shows mostly in the cycles. They are shorter, we know why we commit to them, and at the end we can see what was delivered and for whom. I follow the whole chain, from need to task, without watching over experts who know better than I do how to split their work.

Backlog tools track tickets without knowing why they exist. Product discovery tools link needs and features, with a per-user subscription, but without blocking entry into development. I chose few screens and few fields, with rules that actually block.

The framework, link by link

Persona and epic are the two roots. Under a persona come their pain points and jobs, and each job declares the pain it solves. Under an epic come features, under a feature stories, under a story tasks. A feature also declares the job it serves. The board below replays this chain on the demo project, then the rules that protect it.

The parent-link rules live in the database. PostgreSQL triggers reject a story without a feature, or a feature attached to another feature, whichever screen or import sends it. The other guardrails are checked by the application when a card changes status: a feature with impact 0, or with no job, cannot move to “in progress”, nor can its stories, and the Kanban board accepts no more than five stories in progress.

Three hands, three inputs

Nobody enters both value and cost. Product assigns the feature an impact, from 0 (waste) to 3 (vital). Tech estimates each story in days (½, 1, 2, 3 or 5) and in risk (known, likely, unknown). The epic gets no input, so nobody can inflate it by hand. The system adds up what the other two declared, then divides.

$$\mathrm{Ratio}_{V/E}=\frac{\sum \mathrm{Impact}_{\mathrm{features}}}{\sum \mathrm{Days}_{\mathrm{stories}}}$$

Risk does not enter the ratio. It works as a warning: an unknown risk calls for a spike, a short technical exploration, before any commitment. And the choice of days stops at five; beyond that, the story is badly split. Going against the ratio is still possible, you just have to explain why.

Demo: Panier Local

To show the tool without exposing client data, I filled it with a project made up from scratch. Panier Local connects market gardeners with school canteens. Three personas carry it.

  • Marion, an organic grower on 8 ha, who throws away unsold produce in peak season and finds public procurement baffling.
  • Karim, chef at a secondary school serving 600 meals, who plans menus three weeks ahead with €2.10 of food per meal.
  • Sophie, a buyer for 42 schools, who works out her EGalim rate (France’s quota of quality and organic food in public catering) by hand once a year.

In all, 5 pain points, 6 jobs, 3 epics, 9 features and 19 stories, 13 of them planned over 3 cycles, for a team of three people, fictional as well.

The ratio has an immediate consequence. “Pilotage EGalim”, the only epic not yet started, has the best value for effort, with 5 impact points for 11 days, against 7 for 19 and 7 for 17 for the other two. The tool does not decide, but the question has to come up when the cycle is planned.

Cycles and Kanban

Stories go into fixed-capacity cycles, in the spirit of Shape Up. The Kanban board has only four columns (To do, In progress, In review, Done). Each card shows its feature and epic, so when you move it you always see who you are working for.

Two flaws planted on purpose

I planted two flaws in the project to see whether the tool would find them. The first is a feature with impact 0, attached to an epic but to no need. The hygiene Radar classifies it as both waste and orphan, and the Health Copilot, a panel in the bottom-right corner of every project screen, flags it too. These are rules, not an AI model.

The second is a job no feature serves: Sophie wants to compare local prices with wholesale prices. The dependency graph shows it, with its filter for jobs without a feature. In a real project, the first would have cost time for nothing, and the second would be a need that slipped through. I would rather find both before committing to a cycle.

Under the hood: the stack

A Next.js application on Supabase. The main choice: the parent-link rules live in PostgreSQL, not in the interface. A CSV import, a script or a new screen therefore go through the same checks. Access is partitioned by project, at the level of database rows, and every change to an object is logged.

What STTNS does not do

STTNS does not decide for the team, and it does not check that a pain is real; it only makes sure everything traces back to a declared pain. Committing to a cycle, running a spike or dropping an idea remains our call.

It does not yet measure, after a cycle, whether the announced impact happened. That is the next piece of work. Nor does it plug into an existing ticketing tool: you import and export CSV, and it becomes the reference backlog. Finally, it needs Supabase, so there is no online demo for now.

Try it on your machine

No online demo. STTNS relies on Supabase: the demo runs locally, in Docker, with the Panier Local project reloaded exactly as shown. The full code and setup steps are on my GitHub. 

supabase start            # PostgreSQL, Auth, Storage in Docker
supabase db reset         # installs the schema
cp .env.example .env.local  # copy the keys from: supabase status -o env
docker compose -f docker-compose.local.yml --env-file .env.local up -d --build
./scripts/demo/seed-panier-local.sh you@example.com   # after creating your account

Then open http://localhost:3001.
