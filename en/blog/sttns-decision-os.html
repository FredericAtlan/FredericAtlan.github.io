STTNS — starting from persona needs to decide what gets coded

Every week I have to decide where a few experts spend their time. At Data Refiners, a spreadsheet was no longer enough and ticketing tools asked too much of us. So I built STTNS: every story traces back to the pain of a real user, Product scores the value, the experts estimate the cost, and the tool does the division. The code is public.

In short

  • What. An open-source product steering tool where nothing gets coded unless it traces back to a declared need.
  • For whom. A CTO or engineering manager who takes priorities from a CEO or a product manager.
  • Adopting it. The code is on GitHub and the demo runs locally in Docker. You import your existing backlog as CSV: STTNS replaces it rather than plugging into it.

The starting point: where the experts’ time goes

In a spreadsheet, I kept losing the thread. Who asked for this function, for which customer, with what expected benefit? Ticketing tools track a ticket’s progress very well, its reason for existing much less so, and configuring them would have taken more time than we had. I also needed a teaching tool, so that working from personas and their jobs-to-be-done (JTBD, what the user is trying to get done) showed up on screen instead of staying a principle stated in meetings.

I first applied the Straight to the North Star method, hence the name. The single metric did not hold. The value of a week of expert work does not boil down to one number. We kept JTBD, Shape Up-style cycles and the work-in-progress limit. We dropped the single metric, and with it the reach and confidence of the usual scoring grids. The North Star still exists in the tool, as an optional page, but we no longer steer by it.

The services we owe our personas

The real value, for us, was anchoring ourselves to the services we owe our personas. That is what shapes the product, and what ties the code to real needs. With that chain in front of us, we rank ideas, sort concepts, and start with what really matters.

The split of roles followed. The CEO or product manager scores the features’ impact. The experts estimate their stories in days and risk. I follow the chain from need to task without having to watch closely over people who know better than I do how to break down their work. I measured nothing: I am reporting what the team observed.

The manifesto, in a few lines

STTNS rests on a manifesto that came out of the same discussions. What costs an ambitious product the most is time spent on what nobody wants, and every line written will have to be maintained. An idea with no identified pain, or no demonstrable value, is therefore frozen or dropped, instead of waiting at the bottom of a backlog.

Golden rule: no pain, no product. No validation, no code.

Two human filters come before anything is entered in the tool, hence their number zero. Filter 0 checks that a pain is real:

  • Can it be stated in one sentence, without jargon?
  • Does it come up often in the persona’s daily life?
  • Does it cost something you can put a figure on, in time, money or trust?
  • Does solving it bring the product closer to its promise?

Filter 0 bis applies to sensitive domains, such as accounting, audit or KYC compliance. You may prototype there to learn, but nothing ships without an expert’s validation.

The chain: every story traces back to a pain

It always starts with a person and what gets in their way. Under a persona come their pains and jobs; under an epic, the features, then the stories, then the tasks. Every feature names the job it serves. The board replays this chain on the demo project, then what the tool refuses and what it flags.

What the tool refuses

  • A story without a feature is rejected by a PostgreSQL trigger, whichever screen or import sends it.
  • A feature without a job is refused by the application, on creation as on edit.
  • A feature with impact 0 cannot move to “in progress”, and neither can its stories.
  • Past five stories in progress (the project’s default setting), the Kanban blocks.

The tool’s Playbook puts it in one sentence: “Two hands never enter the same score.” Product does not set the days, the experts do not set the impact, and the epic receives no input, so nobody can inflate it by hand.

$$\mathrm{Ratio}_{V/E}=\frac{\sum \mathrm{Impact}_{\mathrm{features}}}{\sum \mathrm{Days}_{\mathrm{stories}}}$$

A feature with no story yet counts its own days. That is the case of the gamified ranking, which explains the 11 days of the EGalim reporting epic below.

Objections come quickly. Split a feature to inflate impact? Each piece must name the job it serves, and Product does the scoring. A 0-to-3 scale, too coarse? That is deliberate: zero exists to say no plainly. Risk left out of the ratio? An unknown risk cannot be weighted, it calls for a spike, a short technical exploration before any commitment.

The ratio compares epics with one another; it does not decide. The Playbook reads it with three verbs: commit when impact is high and risk known or likely, learn when impact is uncertain or risk unknown, drop when impact is zero or the job cannot be found.

Demo on a fictional project: Panier Local

To show the tool without exposing customer data, I filled it with an invented project. Panier Local connects market gardeners with school canteens, with three personas: Marion, a grower who throws away her unsold produce at the height of the season; Karim, a canteen chef who plans his menus three weeks ahead; Sophie, a buyer for 42 secondary schools, who computes her EGalim rate (the French law on local and organic sourcing) by hand. Of its 19 stories, 13 are planned over 3 cycles.

Two planted defects, two defects found

I slipped two defects into the project. The first is an impact-0 feature, “Gamified school ranking”, born in a workshop and tied to no need. It cannot move to “in progress”. The hygiene Radar classifies it as waste and as an orphan, and the Health Copilot flags it on the project’s screens. These are written rules, with no language model.

The second is a job no feature serves: Sophie wants to compare local prices with wholesale prices. The dependency graph brings it out with its filter for jobs without a feature. The first defect would have cost time for nothing; the second was a need that had fallen through the cracks.

Under the hood: the rules live in PostgreSQL

A Next.js application on Supabase. The parent-link rules are PostgreSQL triggers: a CSV import or a script is subject to them just like a screen. The mandatory job and the status guardrails are checked by the application. Access is isolated per project at the database row level, and every change to a backlog item is logged.

Limits

STTNS does not judge whether a pain is real: that is the job of the first filter, which stays human. The tool checks that everything ties back to a declared need, and flags what does not trace back to a pain. It does not yet measure, after a cycle, whether the announced impact happened. The Playbook asks you to justify a choice that goes against the ratio, but the tool does not store that justification yet. Finally, it does not plug into an existing ticketing tool: you import and export CSV, and it becomes the reference backlog.

Try it on your machine

No online demo. STTNS relies on Supabase: the demo runs locally, in Docker, with the Panier Local project reloaded identically. The full code and the steps to follow are on my GitHub.
